
# Security and Risk Management E-Portfolio


## 1. Portfolio overview

The Pampered Pets group project is the core of this e-portfolio and shows how I’ve grown throughout the module. That project looked at the security, how things run, and supply chain dangers for a small shop that’s starting to use digital technology, online selling, customer relationship management, saving/hosting on the internet (the cloud), and getting goods from other countries. In the final report, the team began with immediate worries like phishing, ransomware, a simple network setup and not being able to bounce back from problems, and then moved on to a more organised look at digital change and changes to the supply chain, using **ISO 31000, ISO/IEC 27005, NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 and STRIDE** (ISO, 2022; Microsoft, 2022; NIST, 2012).

## 2. Individual contributions to the projects

I’m primarily responsible for four things in the Pampered Pets project. I did the research and checked where everything came from, but instead of using a summary of summaries, I went to the actual official advice and standards that back up the report’s points. Specifically I used DSIT for the current threat landscape (DSIT, 2025), the ICO’s requirements for keeping data safe (ICO, 2023), the NCSC’s advice for small and medium businesses to manage security (NCSC, 2025) and NIST/ISO material for the assessment method (NIST, 2012).

Also, I improved how the report is put together by treating **current-state risks** from **digitalisation risks** and **supply-chain risks**, which made the logic of the recommendations clearer.

I also worked on what to do to fix things and the recommendations. I’m thinking particularly of where the security work turns into something a business can do, like swapping out the old computer in the warehouse, needing two-factor authentication, separating personal devices from work, using someone else to manage payments, and thinking of getting things from overseas as a small, carefully watched test – not just a quick way to save money. 

Finally, I went over the report many times, improving the language, getting the order of importance of issues more focused, and including what people I was working with said, so the final report is shorter, has more proof, and helps people make choices.

  
## 3. Evaluation of the final project (Unit 11) versus the status document (Unit 6)

If you look at the Unit 11 final project and the Unit 6 status document for Pampered Pets, you’ll see how the work went from a sort of ‘where things are right now’ update to a full assessment of the risks and suggestions for what to do. The final version of the work has a much more defined scope, it spells out exactly how it was done, and the risk analysis is divided into sections on how things are now, moving to digital systems and the international supply chain, instead of just being a general overview. What's more, the final report is much better at showing how the evidence led to decisions; it uses a clear chart of how likely and how serious something is, names specific risks like phishing, ransomware, badly set up software as a service, problems with managing the online shop, and failures in tracing where something comes from, and then links these issues to a plan to do things in stages and six actions to be done first (ISO, 2022; NIST, 2012).

## 4. Reflective section


I’m using Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper’s (2001) “What? So what? Now what?” approach to reflection. It's useful to me because it gets me to go further than simply describing what happened, and to explain how the Pampered Pets project altered my understanding of security and risk management. 


**What?** The main project for this course was the group work of identifying risks for Pampered Pets. This is a smaller shop looking at becoming more digital, using online sales, cloud computing, a Customer Relationship Management system and an international food chain. The finished report wasn't only a list of possible dangers. It defined the boundaries of the assessment, selected a way of working, found the things needing protection (assets), the dangers to those things (threats), and weaknesses that could be exploited (vulnerabilities). It then ranked the risks, connected them to how to lessen them, and what would have to happen before those lessening steps were put in place. I was most involved in creating the evidence used, making the way the information was organised better, and changing general security worries into prioritized business choices. I worked on moving from a vague discussion of “cyber risk” to a more careful system separating risks in the current situation from those related to digitalisation and the supply chain. I also helped write the parts of the report that described the retailer as a small business with actual day-to-day problems, rather than just a theoretical example. This is important because a little shop doesn't need the highest possible level of security everywhere; instead, it needs security that is appropriate for its size, implemented in a certain order and with a good system of governance.


I used to think of security at the beginning of the course as mostly a technical issue. I’d look for the “best” control first, not the most sensible decision for the situation. But working on Pampered Pets changed that. The project showed that whether a security measure is good or not depends on what you are protecting, how likely something is to happen, what the effects would be, how much money is available, how good the business is at operating, and what the law says. For instance, replacing the old computer in the warehouse, keeping personal devices separate from work ones, adding multi-factor authentication and checking that the backups work weren’t just "good practice”, they were the most important things to do because they dealt with the most likely and most damaging risks at the moment. However, getting goods from abroad seemed good financially, but the report showed that being able to track where things come from, making sure the quality is good and being able to take products back if needed could use up those savings. This made me realise that risk management is about balancing options and having proof, not just going through a list.


**So what?** was the biggest change in how I thought? It was learning to think about risk in a structured way. NIST SP 800-30 and ISO/IEC 27005 became useful to me - not as theories to mention, but as practical ways to deal with uncertainty. I learned to ask what the asset is, what the threat is, what weakness allows the threat to work and what the effects on the business would actually be. This improved my decision making and stopped me from making statements that sounded like I knew about security but didn't have solid evidence. In the Pampered Pets report this was particularly true in the section about becoming digital. Saying “going online increases cyber risk” would have been too simple. It’s much better to explain that using someone else to handle payments reduces the danger of credit card information being stolen, but weak passwords for administrators, badly set up software as a service or accidentally deleting cloud backups create different risks that need different protection.

And a second thing I learned is that legal and ethical responsibilities are part of planning security, not something to consider afterwards. I kept coming back to the UK GDPR, how we handle customer data, and needing to have suitable technical and organizational security. This made me think of confidentiality, integrity, and availability all at the same time. I used to think of data protection mostly as being about following rules, but now I realize that how long we keep data, who can access it, testing our recovery plans and having plans for incidents aren't just about the law, they are part of day-to-day security. And the same was true for the food supply chain: being able to trace something wasn't just about being efficient, but about customer safety, being responsible and what people think of us. This broadened my view of security from being only about computers to being about how people and technology fit together.

Equally important was the team work. I was helpful when I was specific, had proof for what I said and was practical, but less so when I waited to say something because I wanted my ideas to be perfectly formed. In a group, being too hesitant can hold things up. I found that if I shared an initial plan, a first attempt at a paragraph or a suggestion for something to read, the team could all make it better as a unit. When I didn't say anything, I wasn't being as collaborative, even if I was doing it to make sure my work was good. It was a little uncomfortable to realise this, because it went against how I normally decide if a piece of work is 'good'. This course showed me that in a team developing something, being professional includes giving your input quickly, responding to what others say and making it easier for the others to continue building on your work. 

The feedback I got also changed my writing. At the beginning of the module, I would go into a lot of detail about the context before I stated the decision itself. As time went on, I got better at starting with the risk, explaining why it was important, then suggesting a sensible way of dealing with it. The phased “security gates” were particularly useful as they tied the security analysis in with the order of how things would be done. This made the report more about making a decision, and more suitable for a small or medium sized business.


**Now what?** In the future I would do things in four different ways. Firstly, I would decide the way to model the risk and what the standards for decision making are at the start of a project, so everyone on the team knows what “high”, “medium” and “low” mean before any recommendations are made. Secondly, I would write down my assumptions more clearly, particularly when we don't have all the information, as assumptions that aren't shown weaken the analysis. Thirdly, I would contribute earlier to team conversations, even if my ideas aren't fully formed, because working together improves the first draft. And fourthly, I would continue to link security measures to what the business is trying to achieve. The best bits of this module were when security, being able to recover from problems, being legal and being able to operate in the real world were considered as one problem instead of being separate.

  

In general, the Pampered Pets project changed both how I think about the technical side of things and how I behave professionally. I finished the module with a much better grasp of how to assess risk in a structured way, security within a supply chain, choosing what basic controls should be and the connection between cybersecurity and how things are governed. And, perhaps more importantly, I am much more aware of how I work with other people: what I am good at, when I hesitate, and how I can get better. This is why the module has been so useful. It didn't just give me more information about security risks, it taught me how to think about them carefully, explain them in a clear way and be more effective as a member of a team.
  

## 6. Conclusion

  
This e-portfolio as a whole demonstrates that I’ve grown in more than just technical skills during this module. With the Pampered Pets project, I got better at finding proof of things, doing a sensible look at risks, working with others, judging where information comes from, and thinking about what I’ve done. What I improved on most is moving from chatting about security in general, to carefully assessing risks and connecting dangers, weaknesses, how those weaknesses affect the business, and how to reduce the dangers. I’m also now more able to be a useful member of a team, to act on what people tell me to improve, and to give security advice in a way that helps people decide what to do.
